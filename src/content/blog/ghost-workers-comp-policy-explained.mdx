If you've ever been asked to show proof of workers compensation insurance before a contractor would let you on a job site—but you have no employees—you've already run into the problem a ghost workers comp policy solves.

A ghost policy is one of the most misunderstood insurance products in the construction and contracting world. It's neither a scam nor a loophole. It's a legitimate, state-filed insurance product that serves a genuine need. This guide explains exactly what it is, who needs it, how to get one, and where it can get you into trouble.

## What Is a Ghost Workers Comp Policy?

A ghost workers comp policy—also called a ghost policy or named-only policy—is a workers compensation insurance policy that covers the named insured (typically a sole proprietor or small business owner) but lists zero payroll and zero employees. The policy is "real" in that it is issued by a licensed insurer, files the required certificate with the state, and generates a valid Certificate of Insurance (COI) that you can hand to a general contractor or project owner.

The reason it's called a "ghost" policy is that it covers a business with no employees. There are no workers attached to the policy. The premium is minimal because there is no payroll to base rates on—in most states, you pay only the minimum annual premium plus administrative fees.

Ghost policies are issued in all 50 states, though the rules and pricing vary significantly. They are legal where offered, and insurance carriers that file these products have obtained state approval to do so.

## Who Needs a Ghost Workers Comp Policy?

The short answer: anyone who is legally required to carry workers compensation but has no employees.

**Sole Proprietors in the Construction Trades**

Most states exempt sole proprietors from mandatory workers comp coverage for themselves. However, general contractors and commercial property owners often require proof of workers comp from every subcontractor they hire—regardless of whether those subcontractors have employees. A sole proprietor plumber, electrician, or roofer who wants to land commercial jobs or work for larger GCs needs to be able to produce a COI. A ghost policy is how they do it.

**Small LLCs and Corporations with No Employees**

An LLC or corporation with only owner-officers and no W-2 employees typically doesn't have a mandatory workers comp obligation—but again, the companies they want to work with do require it. Ghost policies fill this gap.

**Subcontractors Required to Waive Exclusions**

Some states allow corporate officers to exempt themselves from workers comp coverage. But a GC's policy may require all subs to carry their own coverage without exclusions. A ghost policy issued to the LLC satisfies that contractual requirement.

**Temporary Business Gaps**

A contractor who laid off all employees but still needs to maintain bidding relationships may use a ghost policy to keep a COI active until they hire again.

## How Does a Ghost Policy Actually Work?

Here's the mechanics:

1. **You apply** to an insurance carrier or broker specializing in ghost policies. You provide your business information, license number, and state of operations.
2. **The carrier issues a policy** with zero payroll. The premium is calculated at the minimum premium for the state—often $500–$1,200 per year.
3. **You receive a Certificate of Insurance** naming you as the insured and listing workers compensation coverage. You provide this to GCs, project owners, or whoever requires it.
4. **At audit time** (most WC policies audit annually), the carrier reviews your payroll. If it's still zero, no additional premium is owed. If you hired employees during the year without reporting it, you face a significant retroactive premium charge.

## Ghost Policy Cost Breakdown by State

Ghost policy premiums vary by state because each state has its own minimum premium rules and rate filings:

**Lower-cost states (typically $500–$800/year):**
- Arizona, Nevada, Colorado, Idaho, and many Mountain West states

**Mid-range states (typically $800–$1,200/year):**
- Texas (where WC is not mandatory for most employers, so ghost policies serve a slightly different purpose), Georgia, Tennessee, North Carolina, South Carolina

**Higher-cost states (typically $1,200–$2,500/year):**
- Florida, California, New York, New Jersey, Illinois. These states have higher minimum premiums and more regulatory requirements.

**Monopolistic states** — North Dakota, Ohio, Washington, and Wyoming operate state-run WC funds. Ghost policies in these states work differently and may not be available from private carriers. You must deal directly with the state fund.

Always ask your broker about the exact minimum premium before applying. Some carriers add administrative fees on top of the state minimum, and these vary widely.

## The Application Process

Getting a ghost policy is straightforward if you work with a broker who handles them regularly:

1. **Gather your documents** — business license, EIN or SSN (for sole proprietors), state contractor's license if applicable, and prior insurance history.
2. **Contact a specialist broker** — not every agency handles ghost policies. Look for brokers who work primarily with contractors and construction businesses.
3. **Complete a short application** — because there's no payroll, underwriting is minimal. Most applications ask about your trade, state of operations, and whether you have any prior WC claims.
4. **Bind coverage** — many ghost policies can be bound within 24–48 hours. You receive your policy documents and a COI electronically.
5. **Annual renewal** — ghost policies renew annually. Your broker should remind you when renewal is approaching.

## Common Mistakes Employers Make With Ghost Policies

**Mistake #1: Hiring Employees Without Notifying Your Carrier**

This is the most serious mistake. If you hire even one W-2 employee while your ghost policy is in force and don't notify your carrier, you now have employees with no actual workers comp coverage. If one of those employees gets injured, you face uninsured liability—and you may be personally liable for medical bills, lost wages, and legal fees. You'll also face a significant retroactive premium charge when the carrier audits you.

**Mistake #2: Using a Ghost Policy to Bypass Legitimate Coverage Requirements**

Some contractors get a ghost policy, then subcontract all their labor to 1099 workers, thinking they've satisfied the coverage requirement. This is legally dangerous. If those 1099 workers are later reclassified as employees—which happens frequently after audits—you're retroactively exposed.

**Mistake #3: Purchasing a Ghost Policy from an Unlicensed Provider**

There are fraudulent "certificate mills" that sell fake certificates of insurance. These are not ghost policies—they are fraud. Always verify that your ghost policy is issued by a carrier licensed in your state. Ask your broker for the carrier's NAIC number and verify it on your state's insurance department website.

**Mistake #4: Assuming One Ghost Policy Covers Multiple States**

If you do work in multiple states, you typically need a ghost policy endorsed for each state where you operate. A Florida policy doesn't automatically satisfy a New York GC's insurance requirement.

## When a Ghost Policy Is NOT Enough

A ghost policy satisfies the proof-of-insurance requirement. It does NOT provide actual medical or wage-replacement benefits to anyone. That means:

**If you're injured on the job**, a ghost policy doesn't pay your medical bills or replace your income. Sole proprietors who want personal coverage need to either add themselves to a traditional WC policy or purchase an accident and disability policy separately.

**If a GC requires employer's liability (Part B)**, confirm your ghost policy includes it. Most do, but verify the limits. Standard limits are $100K/$500K/$100K—some projects require higher limits.

**If you're bidding government contracts**, some government contracts have stricter insurance requirements than a basic ghost policy satisfies. Review the contract's insurance specifications carefully.

**If you're in a state that mandates actual WC coverage for all workers**, a ghost policy doesn't make you compliant—it only provides a COI. Understand your state's law before relying solely on a ghost policy.

## Is a Ghost Policy Right for You?

If you're a sole proprietor or small business owner in the construction trades who needs a COI to bid jobs and has no employees, a ghost policy is almost certainly the right tool. It's legal, affordable, and widely accepted by GCs across the country.

If you have employees—or plan to hire them soon—you need a standard workers comp policy, not a ghost policy. The risk of having employees uncovered is simply too great.
